The shift

AI agents are already buying. Last holiday season AI and agents stood behind a fifth of everything sold online, and the businesses behind those shoppers are handing over their invoices next. The question is no longer whether AI agents will pay, but whether anyone can see on whose authority.

Agentic commerce, global, $ billion: actual to 2025, forecast to 2030

2024 and 2025: online holiday sales influenced by AI and agents, Salesforce 2024, Salesforce 2025. 2030: agentic commerce spend, Juniper Research forecast; McKinsey puts the same year at $3 to 5 trillion.

The cost of not knowing

This is what happens when the person paying cannot see who is really behind a request. Of the £1.28 billion taken from UK customers last year, £576 million was authorised push payment fraud, up 19% in a year: payments the victims made themselves, in good faith, to someone they could not see. An AI agent sees even less than they did, and since 2024 the sending bank carries the loss.

The problem

Today an AI agent's payment looks exactly like a person's. Nothing on it says which agent acted, what it was allowed to do, or who stands behind it. The bank sees an instruction and nothing else.

Identity alone does not fix this. Knowing who an AI agent is tells you nothing about what it may do, and nothing about who answers when it goes wrong.

How it works

Three layers set up once, and a check that runs on every payment. Banks operate it, because they carry the fraud loss and hold the customer relationship. Every payment an AI agent makes then carries provable, revocable authority.

  1. The provider registers the AI product. Once. A filing, not an approval: identity, an accountable principal, insurance and Independent Assurance Evidence. The register does not certify that AI agents are good; it guarantees that someone is accountable when they are bad.
  2. The bank admits the product to its list. Once. The bank, not a regulator, decides which registered products its customers may delegate to. Its commercial risk decision and its fraud control.
  3. The customer signs the mandate. Once, inside its own banking app: which payees, how much, until when. The mandate is the consent artefact.
  4. The bank checks. Every payment. Passport and mandate are verified before any money moves, and a signed audit entry is written either way. A manipulated invoice fails here, visibly.

Why a bank would adopt it

  • Fraud loss

    Invoice redirection and prompt injection fail at the payee check, whatever the invoice says. The mandate names accounts, not names.

  • Defensible evidence

    Every decision is signed, hash-chained and replayable from its recorded inputs. When a payment is disputed, the bank shows what the AI agent was permitted to do and what it tried.

  • Liability clarity

    The provider answers for its filing, the customer for its mandate, the bank for the check. Nobody loses except the attacker.

Where this sits in UK policy

Agent Passport is designed to be adopted by industry and to give regulators what they need without asking them to operate or approve it.

HM Treasury, Modernising Payment Services Regulation
The consultation published on 14 July 2026 (closing 6 October 2026) says in section 3.2 that the Payment Services Regulations 2017 were designed before AI and may not fully facilitate agentic payments. Question 15 asks how authentication, consent and liability provisions must adapt. This prototype is one working answer: the passport addresses authentication of the AI agent, the signed mandate addresses consent, and the evidence trail addresses liability. Read the consultation.
Governance on the Open Banking model
The same consultation describes a not-for-profit Future Entity that sets common standards, industry adopting them through commercial schemes, and the FCA setting guardrails and receiving information without operating the infrastructure. APS 1001 is written as a candidate standard for a body of that kind, on the precedent of the Open Banking Implementation Entity. Participation would be commercial, not mandated.
Testing route: FCA AI Lab, Supercharged Sandbox
The FCA's second Supercharged Sandbox cohort (July to December 2026, 21 firms from 199 applications) names agentic payments as a priority focus, with NayaOne, NVIDIA and Anthropic among its partners. The FCA's stated approach is to rely on existing frameworks and use practical experimentation to inform future supervisory expectations. That is the route we intend for testing this design. The sandbox is where a design is tested, not a body that approves a product.
